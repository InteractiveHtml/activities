AIRM: Attachment Injury Repair Model

Tap a step to see what it involves and what it can sound like in practice.

Wounded partner
Wounding partner
Both partners
1

The wounded partner tells their partner how the injury hurt them, explaining both the feeling and why it hurt.

"I felt abandoned."
"I felt humiliated and small."
"I felt completely alone."
2

The wounded partner goes further, identifying the attachment pain and unmet attachment need underneath the feeling.

"I needed you beside me, not to be alone, and I felt gutted in your absence."
"I needed to know that I mattered to you, and I felt worthless thinking I didn't."
"I needed to feel like we were facing this together, and felt lonely and isolated instead."
3

The wounding partner hears, acknowledges and validates the impact of the injury, with genuine patience, empathy and ownership. They demonstrate an understanding of the impact of the injury.

"I can see that you felt abandoned when you needed me the most, and I was not there for you. I am truly sorry that I hurt you. I am here for you now, and I want you to know that you matter to me and that I am not going anywhere."
4

The wounding partner moves away from defensiveness, minimising, justifying and explaining, and takes ownership. They allow repeated conversations where their partner's feelings are heard and validated.

"I am sorry that I hurt you, and I know it will take time for you to trust me again. But I am here for you, and you can talk about this with me as often as you need."
"I did the wrong thing, and I am committed to rebuilding your trust. I know it might take some time."
5

Must express genuine regret and remorse, not impatience for the pain to be resolved.

"I did the wrong thing, and I am committed to rebuilding your trust."
"I'm sorry I hurt you and rocked your world. I can now see that what I did made you feel like your life was turned upside down."
Common errorThe wounding partner becoming impatient for the pain to be resolved and begins to express this:"I have said I am sorry, how many times do we have to go through this.""What more do you want from me, I can't change my actions."
6

Reassurance is provided and direct action is taken to repair, not just words.

"I wish I was there for you."
"You matter to me more than anyone else."
"I have made an appointment to see a psychologist, and I am starting AA."
"I can arrange couples therapy for us, if you would like."
7

Once they can hear the remorse, the wounded partner accepts the apology. This takes bravery and vulnerability, as it means lowering their guard.

The acceptance allows them to slowly lower their protective walls and ask for what they need going forward. In doing this they risk the possibility of their partner not being there for them or responding to their needs.

When the wounding partner responds with care and commitment, it helps to restore a loving bond and communicates "I am here for you, you matter to me".

8

This step is ongoing. It requires consistency, transparency and reliability from both partners over time.
